# Clarity study: how a wordless film keeps the want unmistakable

- **Sources:** four Alan Becker films, studied as a first-time viewer with
  vidIQ's long-form visual analysis on 2026-10-14. Nothing was downloaded.
  - "Animation vs. Math" (`B1J6Ou4q8vE`)
  - "Animation vs. Physics" (`ErMSHiQRnc8`)
  - "Animator vs. Animation", the original (`npTC6b5-yvM`)
  - "Animation vs. Minecraft", the original (`GW_fdXHOWp4`)
- **Scope:** technique only, written in our own words. No characters,
  designs or jokes are to be reused.
- **Caveat:** the timestamps are approximate, as reported by the analysis.

## 1. What all four films do in the first 10 seconds of every beat

**The goal and the character are in the same frame.** The want is never
an idea in the character's head. It is an *object on screen* that the
character turns toward, reaches for, walks to, or is blocked from:

| film | the want, as an object | how it is shown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Math | a single "1" on the floor | looks down at it, reaches, picks it up |
| Physics | a glowing ball that falls nearby; later, a far bright point | head turns to it, touches it, follows it; later, a straight line of travel toward the point |
| Animator vs. Animation | the cursor above | the figure glares up at it, aims at it, throws at it |
| Minecraft | an app icon, then a beacon, then the villain | steps on the icon; guards the beacon; raises swords and marches at a folder |

**Gaze → reach → move.** Intent is a three-part pantomime: the eyes go to
the goal first, then the hand, then the body. The audience follows the
eyes.

## 2. Every scene is a small loop

**want → try → result → reaction → new want**

The *reaction* is the part beginners skip, and it is the part that tells
the audience what just happened and how to feel:

- **Failure reactions:** slump and sit (Math), slide back down the hill
  dejected (Physics), glare up (Animator vs. Animation), inspect the
  damage (Minecraft).
- **Thinking reactions:** a hand to the chin, a head tilt, a still
  pause.
- **Fear reactions:** step back, cower, hide behind something.

Each reaction lasts **12–24 frames (0.2–0.4 s) at the least**, and up to
1–2 s after a big turn. That is long enough for the viewer to read it.

The *new want* always comes out of the result. Math: the sum gets too
long, so the new want is to make it shorter. Physics: the swing falls
short, so the new want is more energy.

## 3. Devices that remove ambiguity

1. **Rule of three:**
   - Physics: the swing falls short, falls short again with more pull,
     then flings far too far.
   - Minecraft: tools go wood, iron, diamond.
   - The third try either wins or overshoots, and the audience feels
     the pattern.
2. **Cause and effect in one shot.** The trigger and the consequence
   are never split by a cut while a rule is being taught.
3. **One action at a time.** While the key action happens, everything
   else holds still (Animator vs. Animation freezes the scene while the
   weapon is drawn).
4. **Show the tool before using it.** An item is seen in a hand or an
   inventory before it changes the world.
5. **Set-up → pay-off pairs:**
   - Physics: the ball that falls at the start returns at the end, and
     so does the hat.
   - Minecraft: the explosive used against the heroes is later used by
     them.
   - Math: the first tear in the background becomes the final escape.
6. **Visual signifiers for duplicates.** When two identical figures
   appear, one wears an accessory (Physics) or holds a tool (Animator vs.
   Animation).
7. **Pull back to explain.** When something confusing starts (blocks
   flying out of a hand), the camera widens to show the whole picture (a
   giant suit being built).
8. **Physicalise the abstract.** Math becomes swords, bows, shields and
   weight. A health bar makes damage readable.

## 4. Why we care

- **Small against big:** a figure dwarfed by planets, or by a cursor that
  can erase it.
- **Playful:** twirling tools, a hat put on with a flourish.
- **Vulnerable:** curling up, panting, weeping on the floor.
- **Success and failure shown to matter:** a slump after failing makes the
  next run-up a cheer. The hold on a defeated character makes the
  outstretched hand land.

## 5. FIRST LIGHT, diagnosed against this

| rule | FIRST LIGHT v1 | problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal in frame | Spark holds a dead bulb up to the dark | no goal object: *why* hold it up? Light for what? |
| Gaze → reach → move | rarely aimed at a target | Spark often looks at nothing in particular |
| The loop's reaction | present at the zap and the pop; thin elsewhere | small successes (the AND, the OR) pass without a read |
| New want from the result | the jump from "light" to "build logic" to "build a learning board" is unmotivated | **the audience doesn't know why Spark builds the machine** |
| Rule of three | the pinch (3×) and the juggle | the attempts that matter (reaching the light, getting it to work) are not threes |
| Cause and effect in one shot | mostly yes | the "accident" (plug into motors) is far across the frame and fast |
| Set-up → pay-off | dead bulb, then violet bulb | good, but the meaning ("it acts without being asked") is weak because the want was weak |
| Physicalise | beads, dials, a filling bulb | good |

**The fix is a concrete, relatable want from the first frame:**
*Spark wants to read a book in the dark.* Everything Spark builds must
serve that want, or the next want that grows out of it.
